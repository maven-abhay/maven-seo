AI for Airlines: 7 Practical Use Cases, From Booking to Disruption

Where artificial intelligence delivers real value for airlines today, how it works, and how to start safely.

8 min read By Manoj K Joshi, CTO & Co-Founder

Airlines run on thin margins, complex rules and huge volumes of customer contact. That makes them one of the industries where artificial intelligence can make the biggest practical difference: not as a futuristic experiment, but as a way to answer travellers faster, automate repetitive servicing work and help teams make better decisions.

Why AI matters for airlines now

Airlines have used algorithms for decades in pricing and revenue management. What has changed is generative AI. Large language models can now understand a traveller’s question, read a fare rule or policy, and write a clear answer in seconds. Combined with traditional machine learning and the APIs that already power booking engines, this opens up use cases that were not practical a few years ago.

The opportunity is not to add a chatbot for its own sake. It is to remove friction at the moments that matter most to travellers and that cost airlines the most to handle: changes, disruptions, refunds and questions about the rules.

Key takeaways

  • The strongest airline AI use cases are in servicing, disruption, refunds and agent support, not just sales.
  • AI must be connected to your booking engine, GDS or NDC and policies to give answers travellers can act on.
  • Ground answers in approved sources, show where they came from, and keep a person in the loop for exceptions.
  • Start with one narrow, measurable use case, prove it, then scale.

Where AI fits in the passenger journey

A useful way to find AI opportunities is to walk through the passenger journey and ask where travellers wait, repeat themselves or get stuck, and where your teams do the same task again and again.

7 practical AI use cases for airlines

These use cases are practical today with current technology. Each one includes a suggested starting point that keeps risk low and makes results easy to measure.

1. Booking and servicing copilots

A conversational assistant on your website, app or messaging channels can help travellers search for flights, compare fares, add bags or seats and change a booking in plain language. The best copilots do not replace the booking engine; they sit on top of it and call the same pricing, availability and payment services, so every answer is real and bookable.

Start with: one high-volume task, such as seat changes or adding a bag, before opening up full booking.

2. Proactive disruption handling

When a flight is delayed or cancelled, travellers want to know what happens next. AI can draft clear, personalised messages, explain the options a traveller is entitled to and guide them to rebook or request a refund in a few taps, instead of queuing at a desk or waiting on the phone.

Start with: clearer, faster disruption messages that link straight to self-service rebooking.

3. Refund and claims automation

Refunds and claims are document-heavy and rule-heavy, which makes them a strong fit for AI. Models can classify requests, read receipts and documents, check them against fare rules and route straightforward cases for automatic processing, while unusual cases go to a person with a summary already prepared.

Start with: the most common refund type, with a human approving every decision until accuracy is proven.

4. Contact-centre agent assist

Agents spend a lot of time searching policies and reading long case histories. An AI assistant can summarise the conversation so far, suggest the correct policy with a link to the source and draft a reply for the agent to check. Agents stay in control, and customers get faster, more consistent answers.

Start with: case summaries and policy search, which save time without changing who makes the decision.

5. Personalised offers and ancillaries

Predictive models can estimate which extras a traveller is likely to want, such as extra legroom, a bag or lounge access, and when to offer them. Generative AI can then write the offer in the right tone for each channel. The result is fewer irrelevant offers and more useful ones.

Start with: one ancillary and one channel, and compare it with your current offer logic.

6. Demand forecasting and revenue analytics

Machine learning models can combine booking curves, seasonality, events and search data to forecast demand more accurately and help revenue teams react sooner. Generative AI adds a new layer on top: analysts can ask questions about the data in plain language and get a chart or a summary back.

Start with: forecasting for a small set of routes, measured against the current method.

7. Knowledge assistants for crew and staff

Crew, ground staff and office teams rely on long manuals, procedures and notices that change often. A secure internal assistant can answer their questions with a direct link to the right section of the source document, so people find the current answer quickly and the manuals stay the single source of truth.

Start with: one set of documents, such as ground-handling procedures, with access limited by role.

How an AI travel assistant finds the right answer

Most of the use cases above rely on a technique called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. Instead of answering from memory, the AI first retrieves the relevant fare rules, policies, booking details and live flight data, then writes an answer based only on that information.

This approach has three big advantages for airlines. Answers reflect your current rules rather than general knowledge. Every answer can show its source, so agents and travellers can check it. And when the rules change, you update the documents, not the model.

Risks and guardrails

AI in a regulated, customer-facing business needs clear guardrails. The most important ones are:

  • Accuracy: test the assistant against real traveller questions before launch, and keep measuring after it goes live.
  • Hand-over: route complex, sensitive or low-confidence cases to a person, with the conversation summarised.
  • Privacy: protect passenger data, limit what the model can see and choose deployment options, such as your own cloud, that meet your data rules.
  • Actions: let the AI suggest actions, such as a rebooking, but confirm them through your existing booking and payment services.
  • Cost: use the right-sized model for each task and monitor usage so costs stay predictable.

How to get started

  1. Pick one use case with clear value and an easy measure, such as the time to resolve a seat-change request.
  2. Check the data and systems it needs: documents, booking data, and the APIs of your booking engine, GDS or NDC.
  3. Build a pilot on your own data and test it with real users and agents.
  4. Evaluate accuracy, cost and customer impact against the targets you set, then decide whether to scale.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best first AI project for an airline?

Choose a task that is frequent, rule-based and easy to measure, such as answering common servicing questions, summarising cases for contact-centre agents or processing simple refunds. These show value quickly and carry low risk when a person stays in the loop.

Will AI replace airline contact-centre agents?

Not in the projects that work well. AI handles routine questions and prepares information, while agents handle complex, sensitive or high-value cases with better context and less searching.

How do you stop an AI assistant giving travellers wrong information?

Ground every answer in approved sources such as fare rules, policies and live booking data, show where the answer came from, test the assistant against real questions before launch and hand over to a person when it is not confident.

Does AI need to connect to our booking engine and GDS or NDC systems?

For anything beyond general questions, yes. To change a booking, check a fare or process a refund, the assistant must call the same services your booking engine uses, through secure APIs.

How long does it take to launch an airline AI pilot?

It depends on data readiness and how many systems the pilot must connect to. A focused pilot that uses existing documents or a single integration is much faster than one that touches many systems, so we recommend starting narrow.
